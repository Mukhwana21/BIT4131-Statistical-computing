# Topic guide: theory, mathematics, and interpretation

## Dataset and conventions

The analysis uses 240 synthetic records in `data/student_performance.csv`.
Variables include study group, gender, study hours, attendance percentage,
assessment score, pass status, and date. Scores were generated with a positive
study-hours and attendance relationship, a modest group effect, seasonality,
and random noise. The `score` and `study_hours` columns intentionally contain
missing values; `score_complete` is retained for analyses that require a
complete response. This controlled dataset makes methods demonstrable but does
not support claims about any real population.

Unless stated otherwise, analyses use available complete observations. Numeric
results and plots are generated by the runners, rather than copied as stale
values into this guide. A confidence interval describes uncertainty under its
sampling assumptions; a p-value is not the probability that the null
hypothesis is true.

## 1. Measures of central tendency and dispersion

The mean is \(\bar{x}=\sum_i x_i/n\); the median is the central order
statistic; the mode is the most frequent value. Sample variance is
\(s^2=\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})^2/(n-1)\), and standard deviation is \(s=\sqrt{s^2}\).
Quartiles divide ordered data into four parts. The example calculates these
summaries for `score_complete` and visualizes spread with a boxplot. Compare
the mean and median to assess skew, and use the IQR to describe the middle half.

## 2. Probability and conditional probability

For events \(A,B\), \(P(A\mid B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)\), provided \(P(B)>0\).
The empirical example estimates the chance of passing overall and within group
B, then simulates Bernoulli outcomes using the observed pass rate. Simulation
frequencies should approach the empirical probability as the number of trials
increases.

## 3. Probability distributions

The normal density is \(f(x;\mu,\sigma)=
\exp[-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)]/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})\). Estimate \(\mu,\sigma\)
from observed scores, calculate a probability below a selected threshold, and
compare the fitted curve with a score histogram. The normal fit is a
descriptive approximation; bounded or non-normal scores may not follow it well.

## 4. Sampling distributions and the Central Limit Theorem

For independent observations with finite variance, the sample mean has
\(E(\bar X)=\mu\) and \(SE(\bar X)=\sigma/\sqrt{n}\); its distribution becomes
approximately normal as \(n\) grows. Repeated samples of size 30 demonstrate
the sampling distribution and its standard error. Compare the simulated mean
and spread with the dataset mean and \(s/\sqrt{30}\).

## 5. Confidence intervals

For a mean with unknown population standard deviation, a \(100(1-\alpha)\%\)
one-sample interval is \(\bar{x}\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-1}s/\sqrt n\). The example
uses a 95% t interval for the mean complete score. In repeated sampling,
intervals constructed this way would cover the population mean about 95% of
the time under the model assumptions.

## 6. Hypothesis testing

The one-sample t statistic is \(t=(\bar{x}-\mu_0)/(s/\sqrt n)\), with \(n-1\)
degrees of freedom. Test \(H_0:\mu=60\) against a two-sided alternative for
complete scores at \(\alpha=0.05\). Report the statistic, p-value, decision,
and practical context; failure to reject is not proof of equality.

## 7. Correlation and covariance

Sample covariance is \(s_{xy}=\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})/(n-1)\);
Pearson correlation is \(r=s_{xy}/(s_xs_y)\). The example examines study hours
and score using complete pairs, a covariance/correlation matrix, and a
scatterplot. Correlation describes linear association and does not establish
causation.

## 8. Linear regression and least squares

The model is \(Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1 x_{i1}+\beta_2 x_{i2}+\epsilon_i\), with
coefficients chosen to minimize \(\sum_i(Y_i-\hat Y_i)^2\). Regress complete
score on study hours and attendance. Interpret slopes conditionally on the
other predictor, and report coefficients, p-values, \(R^2\), residual checks,
and fitted values. The model is associational, not causal.

## 9. Matrix algebra in statistics

For compatible matrices, addition is elementwise and multiplication is
\((AB)_{ij}=\sum_k A_{ik}B_{kj}\). The transpose swaps rows and columns; the
determinant indicates whether a square matrix is invertible. Solve \(Ax=b\)
and verify the residual \(Ax-b\) is near zero. The example also computes
\((X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty\) for the regression design matrix when it is full rank.

## 10. Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and PCA

For covariance matrix \(S\), eigenvectors \(v_j\) satisfy \(Sv_j=\lambda_jv_j\).
PCA uses these directions to represent maximal variance; explained variance
ratio is \(\lambda_j/\sum_k\lambda_k\). Standardize study hours, attendance,
and complete score before PCA because they use different units. Inspect the
scree plot and component scores; components are summaries, not causal factors.

## 11. Numerical optimization

Least squares minimizes \(J(\beta_0,\beta_1)=\sum_i(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i)^2\).
An iterative optimizer starts from an initial guess and seeks a minimum. Fit
the score-versus-study-hours line and report the coefficients, objective,
and convergence status. Compare the result with the closed-form least-squares
estimate to check numerical accuracy.

## 12. Random numbers and Monte Carlo methods

Monte Carlo approximates an expectation by an average of simulated outcomes.
For uniform points in the unit square, the proportion \(q\) inside the
quarter-circle gives \(\hat\pi=4q\). Its standard error is approximately
\(4\sqrt{q(1-q)/N}\). A convergence plot shows how the estimate changes as
more draws are included.

## 13. Time-series analysis

A series \(Y_t\) can be decomposed into trend, seasonal, and irregular
components. The example aggregates mean score by month, plots the series, and
reports a linear trend and autocorrelation. A simple next-period forecast
extends the estimated trend; with only two years of synthetic observations,
seasonality and forecast uncertainty should be treated cautiously.

## 14. Statistical data visualization

Visualizations should match variable types and the question: histogram for
numeric distribution, boxplot for group comparisons, scatterplot for two
numeric variables, and line chart for measurements over time. Axes, units,
labels, and captions are included. Visual patterns support, but do not replace,
statistical analysis.

## 15. Data transformation and normalization

Z-score standardization is \(z_i=(x_i-\bar{x})/s\); min-max normalization is
\(u_i=(x_i-\min x)/(\max x-\min x)\). The example compares original scores,
z-scores, and values on \([0,1]\). Standardization preserves relative distances
under a linear transformation, while min-max scaling is sensitive to extremes.

## 16. ANOVA and experimental design

One-way ANOVA tests equality of \(k\) group means with
\(F=MS_{\text{between}}/MS_{\text{within}}\). Analyze complete score by study
group, report the ANOVA table, F statistic, p-value, means, and Tukey pairwise
comparisons. Independence, approximate within-group normality, and comparable
variances are assumptions; group membership was not randomized here.

## 17. Chi-square and categorical data analysis

For observed counts \(O_{ij}\), expected counts under independence are
\(E_{ij}=(\text{row total})(\text{column total})/N\), and
\(\chi^2=\sum_{ij}(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2/E_{ij}\). Test association between study
group and pass status with a contingency table. Check expected cell counts and
interpret an association as non-causal.

## 18. Logistic regression

For binary \(Y\), \(\log[p/(1-p)]=\beta_0+\beta_1x_1+\beta_2x_2\), and
\(\exp(\beta_j)\) is the adjusted odds ratio for a one-unit predictor change.
Predict pass status from study hours and attendance, report coefficients,
odds ratios, confusion matrix, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score.
Performance depends on the selected 0.5 cutoff and this synthetic sample.

## 19. Non-parametric statistical methods

The Mann–Whitney U test compares rank distributions of two independent groups
without requiring normally distributed outcomes. Compare complete scores by
gender using the rank test and, for context, a two-sample t-test. The rank test
does not generally test only equality of medians unless distribution shapes
are similar.

## 20. Bootstrap and resampling methods

The nonparametric bootstrap repeatedly samples \(n\) observations with
replacement and recomputes a statistic. Use bootstrap means to estimate the
sampling distribution, standard error, and percentile 95% interval for mean
complete score. Resampling represents uncertainty in the observed empirical
distribution and cannot repair bias or nonrepresentative data.

## 21. Multivariate statistical analysis

Multivariate analysis studies several variables jointly. The example reports
descriptive and covariance structure for study hours, attendance, and score,
then clusters standardized records into three groups with k-means. Cluster
labels are arbitrary and the result depends on scaling, distance, and chosen
cluster count.

## 22. Missing-data analysis and imputation

For variable \(X\), missingness rate is \(n_{\text{missing}}/n\). The example
reports missing counts, visualizes missingness, imputes numeric values with
the training-sample median, and compares observed and imputed distributions.
Median imputation is a simple demonstration; it understates uncertainty and is
not a universal solution. The reason for missingness should guide method choice.

## 23. Statistical reporting and reproducible analysis

A reproducible report records data provenance, methods, assumptions, numerical
results, graphs, interpretation, and conclusions alongside executable code.
Both runners generate a Markdown report from the same data and fixed seeds.
Re-run the report after changing data or code; distinguish planned analyses
from exploratory findings and state limitations.
